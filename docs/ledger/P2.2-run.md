## How this record got the verdict wrong first, and what settled it

**Four commits reported `floor met`** — `53caf94`, `0ae641b`, `5b708f7` and `66d977d` — on five
entries, with the weakness recorded beside it as a defect in the plan's criterion. The reasoning
was that imposing a distinctness rule after seeing the count would amend a contract frozen at
`731d627` precisely so the rule could not be tuned to the answer. That reasoning is sound and it
was the wrong thing to be careful about.

**What settled it is the count.** MC1's unit is a committed scripted scenario, an `it.each` is one,
and four is below five. No interpretation is needed and no contract is amended. The dilemma those
four commits agonised over was an artifact of miscounting.

**"Floor met" was also never one of MC4's two strings.** It authorises
`inconclusive — insufficient replay corpus` below the floor and, at or above it, a statement that
*the quality gate is evaluable*. Printing "floor met" and withdrawing the licence two sections
later is a third verdict the contract does not have, filed where a reader starting P3.3 would not
look. That is fixed independently of the count.

Whether a corpus of one observation could satisfy "evaluable" at a count of five is where the two
lanes disagreed — Grok read it as a substantive condition, GPT as satisfied by the floor itself.
`replay-set.md` records the disagreement under the verdict and takes no position, because at a
count of four the question does not arise. It will arise the moment a fifth scenario exists, which
is why it is flagged rather than dropped.

**On how the two lanes divided the work, stated accurately because an earlier draft of this
paragraph was not.** It is not that the entry gate confined itself to robustness while the second
lane found the meaning defect. The entry gate found every arithmetic defect in this record — the
mis-applied de-duplication key, both undercounted integration figures, the compressed state
sequence, the off-by-one transition count — and it was asked twice, explicitly, to overturn the
verdict, adjudicated it both times, and upheld it. The mistaken verdict was reviewed and affirmed,
not merely unexamined. Grok then overturned it on its first look. The useful observation is not
about either lane's diligence but about order: the second lane arrives at a commit the first has
already made internally consistent, which is when a wrong top-line claim is easiest to see and
hardest to hide behind an error.
